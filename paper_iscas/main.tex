\begin{abstract}
Diamond magnetometers based on nitrogen-vacancy centers are moving from the laboratory to field
applications such as magnetic anomaly navigation, where a wrong field estimate becomes a wrong
position. The quantum sensor is hard to reach remotely, but its classical microwave control
chain is not. We show that an attacker who offsets the microwave drive frequency by less than
one part per million reproduces the photon statistics of a genuine field shift for any Ramsey
interrogation schedule, so the sensor cannot verify its own output. Secret interrogation timing
stops precomputed phase attacks but not frequency offsets, and driving both spin transitions
analytically reduces a clock error's bias roughly tenfold. We then analyze the common defense of falling
back to an auxiliary reference sensor on alarm. Under attack, the rate of harmful fallback outputs cannot fall below
the reference's own error rate, and a closed-form design equation predicts a blind window of small
spoofs that pass unnoticed, which fixes how precise the reference must be. In a simulation study with 30
independently seeded pipelines and a protocol fixed before the run, the equation matches all
eight tested reference conditions within one percentage point, and a reference that shares the primary sensor's clock releases a harmful
estimate on almost every attack.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Nitrogen-vacancy (NV) magnetometers combine high sensitivity with room-temperature operation,
which makes them candidates for field deployment~\cite{barry2020sensitivity}. One such
application is magnetic anomaly navigation, where the measured field is matched against a map to
obtain position~\cite{canciani2016magnav}. In this setting a field error is a navigation error,
and the integrity of the released estimate matters as much as its precision.

An NV Ramsey magnetometer reads the field as a phase that accumulates between two microwave
pulses. The spin itself is difficult to reach remotely, but the phase reference is defined by
classical electronics: a reference clock, a microwave synthesizer, an arbitrary waveform
generator, and the cables between them. Signal injection into the analog front ends of sensors
is well documented~\cite{kune2013ghost,shoukry2013abs}. For an NV sensor the required
manipulation is small: a \SpoofOffsetkHz{}\,kHz drive offset, \SpoofOffsetPpm{}\,ppm of the
\UpperLineGHz{}\,GHz transition, imitates a 100\,nT field change, and every tone derived from a
shifted reference clock inherits the clock's fractional error, so pulling the usual 10\,MHz
reference by about 10\,Hz, or rewriting the synthesizer's frequency setting, is enough.

Existing work does not address this threat. NV sensing research treats control errors as benign
imperfections to be averaged out by robust or double-quantum
sequences~\cite{oshnik2022robust,fang2013quantumbeats,hart2021dq}. Spoofing defenses for
classical active sensors hide probing times from the attacker~\cite{shoukry2015pycra}, a strategy
that fast attackers can bypass~\cite{shin2016sampling}. Anomaly detectors are usually judged by
detection rate, although for a field estimate what matters is whether the released value is
correct after an alarm. This paper asks: \emph{if an attacker manipulates the microwave drive of an
NV Ramsey magnetometer, what can the defender detect, and how much of the field estimate can
hardware architecture recover?} We make three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Control-chain attack surface} (Section~\ref{sec:primary}). A drive-frequency offset
is equivalent to a field shift in the photon statistics for any schedule. Secret timing defeats
only precomputed phase attacks, and dual-transition driving analytically reduces the field bias of a
1\,ppm clock error from about \ClockSingleBias{}\,nT to \ClockDualBias{}\,nT at a 10\,mT bias field.
\item \textbf{Reference architecture and design equation} (Section~\ref{sec:reference}). Fallback
to a reference sensor is bounded by the reference's own error. A closed-form equation predicts
the residual harm and a blind window of small spoofs, and gives the reference noise needed to
close it.
\item \textbf{Engineering evaluation and design rules} (Sections~\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:rules}).
A 30-pipeline simulation study quantifies attack success, clock sharing, and the precision,
availability and cost of fallback, abstention and fusion, and maps each countermeasure to the
hardware it requires.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sensor Architecture and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/chain.tex}
\caption{Sensor chain and trust boundaries. The attacker offsets the drive frequency
($\delta\omega$) or analysis phase ($\delta\phi$) after the trusted scheduler; the command log
stays protected. The reference sensor is secure only if it shares no clock, synthesizer or data
path with the primary chain (red dashed path).}
\label{fig:chain}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:chain} shows the architecture. A trusted scheduler chooses interrogation times
$t_k$ and analysis phases $\phi_k$ and records them. The synthesizer and waveform generator turn
these commands into pulses on a single NV transition, the optical readout returns photon counts,
and the estimator computes a field estimate $\Bhat$ from the counts and the logged commands. A
verifier scores held-out settings, and an optional reference sensor supplies an independent
reading $R$ of the same field.

The attacker acts between the scheduler and the spin: it can add a constant drive-frequency
offset or change the analysis phase of each pulse, either from a precomputed table or with
real-time knowledge of the schedule. It cannot observe the true field or the reference and
cannot alter counts, logs, software or the reference path; an attacker with that access controls
the output directly and must be stopped by data-path protection. A release $Y$ is
\emph{harmful} if $|Y-B|>\epsilon_H=25$\,nT, and the attack targets a 100\,nT offset. We also
report coverage (fraction of attempts with a released output) and root-mean-square error (RMSE)
over released outputs.

\section{What the Primary Sensor Can Reveal}
\label{sec:primary}

With preparation phase $\beta$, analysis phase $\phi$ and spin-drive detuning $\delta\omega$, the
probability of reading $m_s=0$ after a Ramsey sequence is~\cite{degen2017quantum,rondin2014magnetometry}
\begin{equation}
 p=\tfrac12\bigl[1+(1-2\eta)\,c\,e^{-t/\Ttwo}\cos\Theta\bigr],\quad
 \Theta=(\gamma B+\delta\omega)t+\beta-\phi,
 \label{eq:ramsey}
\end{equation}
with gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma$, contrast $c$, dephasing time $\Ttwo$ and symmetric readout error $\eta$; $N$ shots at each setting
give binomial counts.

\textbf{Frequency offsets are invisible.} The field enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:ramsey} only through the
net detuning $\gamma B+\delta\omega$. More generally, whenever the rotating-frame dynamics of the
addressed transition depend on $B$ only through this detuning, lowering the drive frequency by
$\gamma\Delta B$ produces exactly the count distribution of a field $B+\Delta B$, for any pulse
shape and any schedule, including secret ones. No test on the primary data can then separate the
two, and the attack is undetectable in the sense of~\cite{pasqualetti2013attack}. The condition
neglects the unaddressed transition, counter-rotating terms and the $^{14}$N nuclear spin. A
lab-frame simulation of the coupled electron-nuclear system with finite 25\,ns pulses and no
rotating-wave approximation gives a largest population difference, over eight random settings per
bias field, of
\SpinNominalDiff{}$\times10^{-4}$ at a 10\,mT bias (\SpinMaxDiff{}$\times10^{-4}$ at 5\,mT),
against a median spoof signal of \SpinSignal{} and a per-setting shot noise of \SpinShotNoise{}.
If the residual stays at this level, even a detector that knew it exactly would need at least \DetectSequencesNominal{}
sequences at 10\,mT (\DetectSequencesWorst{} at 5\,mT) to detect it with 50\% power at a 1\%
false-alarm level, so the attack is undetectable within any single sequence.

\textbf{Secret timing stops precomputed phase attacks.} A phase actuator imitates the field shift
only if it applies $\delta\phi_k=-\gamma\Delta B\,t_k$ with the true interrogation time. An
attacker that must commit to a table built for the nominal times $t_{\rm nom}$ leaves a residual
phase $\gamma\Delta B\,(t_{\rm nom}-t_k)$ that varies from setting to setting when the $t_k$ are
drawn secretly, and a held-out likelihood test detects the resulting misfit. A frequency offset
needs no such knowledge, so secret timing does not help against it.

\textbf{Dual-transition driving cancels common clock errors.} With transition frequencies
$\omega_\pm=D\pm\gamma(B_0+B)$ and tone offsets $a_\pm$, the difference of the two measured
detunings contains $2\gamma(B_0+B)-(a_+-a_-)$, so offsets common to both tones and drifts of the
zero-field splitting $D$ cancel~\cite{fang2013quantumbeats,bauch2018ultralong,hart2021dq}. A
fractional clock error $\epsilon$ scales both tones; at $\epsilon=1$\,ppm and $B_0=10$\,mT it
biases a single-transition estimate by about \ClockSingleBias{}\,nT but a dual-transition
estimate by only \ClockDualBias{}\,nT. To spoof the dual-transition sensor, the attacker must
inject a precise differential offset on two tones. This is an algebraic capability bound, not a
demonstrated defense, and a single-transition sensor still needs an external reference.

\section{Reference-Assisted Verification}
\label{sec:reference}

\textbf{Architecture.} Each sequence splits its settings into an estimation set, on which the
maximum-likelihood estimate $\Bhat$ is fitted, and a verification set, on which the negative
log-likelihood score $S_N$ is computed. A second score, $S_R=|\Bhat-R|$, compares the primary
estimate with the reference, modeled as
\begin{equation}
 R=B+b_R+\lambda\Delta B+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_R^2),
 \label{eq:reference}
\end{equation}
where a bias $b_R$ and a coupling $\lambda$ describe imperfect isolation ($b_R=\lambda=0$ for an
independent reference). The alarm fires when either score exceeds its threshold; each threshold is a quantile of clean
calibration scores at level $\alpha/2$, so the clean false-alarm rate targets
$\alpha=1\%$~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}. We compare four output policies built on this
combined alarm: \emph{fallback} releases $R$ on alarm and $\Bhat$ otherwise;
\emph{abstention} releases $\Bhat$ only without alarm; \emph{always-reference} ignores the primary
sensor; and \emph{fusion} rules combine $\Bhat$ and $R$ continuously.

\textbf{The reference sets a floor.} A fallback release is harmless only if an accepted $\Bhat$ or
a substituted $R$ is accurate. When the spoof makes $\Bhat$ harmful, only the second case remains,
so fallback harm is at least the reference's own harm, whatever the alarm rule. The same holds
in the worst case for every fusion rule of the form $Y=R+g(\Bhat-R)$, which includes fallback,
inverse-variance weighting and clipping. For a Gaussian reference and a primary error that does
not depend on the field, when the attacker may choose the spoof size $\Bhat$
carries no usable information about $B$, and worst-case harm cannot fall below the reference
floor $2\Phi(-\epsilon_H/\sigma_R)$.

\textbf{Design equation.} For a spoof $d>h=\epsilon_H$ with an ideal primary estimate
$\Bhat=B+d$, a reference shift $s=b_R+\lambda d$ and discrepancy threshold $\kappa$, fallback harm is
\begin{equation}
 H_F(d)=P(|s+\varepsilon|>h)+P\bigl(|s+\varepsilon|\le h,\ |d-s-\varepsilon|\le\kappa\bigr).
 \label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
The first term is the reference floor; the second counts accurate references that fail to raise
the alarm, so the spoofed $\Bhat$ is released. For an independent reference ($s=0$) and ignoring the likelihood alarm, harm is $1$
in a \emph{blind window} $h<d\le\kappa-h$, falls in $\kappa-h<d<\kappa+h$, and reaches the floor for
$d\ge\kappa+h$. With primary noise $\sigma_P$, the calibrated threshold is approximately
\begin{equation}
 \kappa\approx z_{1-\alpha/4}\sqrt{\sigma_R^2+\sigma_P^2},
 \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
with $z_{1-\alpha/4}=\TheoryZ$ for a two-sided test at level $\alpha/2$. The blind window exists
when $\kappa>2h$; at $\sigma_R=20$\,nT it spans 25 to \TheoryBlindUpper\,nT. For
$\sigma_P\approx\TheorySigmaPrimary$\,nT this happens once $\sigma_R>\TheorySigmaCritical$\,nT, which
turns Eqs.~\eqref{eq:closed} and~\eqref{eq:kappa} into a sizing rule for the reference sensor.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\textbf{Setup.} We simulate the Ramsey model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ramsey} with $\Ttwo=100\,\mu$s,
$\eta=0.1$, five nominal times from 5 to 60\,$\mu$s or secret times drawn uniformly in that
range, two analysis phases, and 1000 shots per setting (20 settings per sequence). Fields are
drawn from $[0,1.9]\,\mu$T, the nominal reference has $\sigma_R=20$\,nT, and the nominal spoof
is 100\,nT. Thirty independently seeded pipelines each calibrate their own thresholds and attacker
tables and evaluate 512 clean/attacked pairs per condition, \TotalN{} attempts in total.
Policies, conditions, endpoints and seeds were fixed and time-stamped before a single evaluation
run. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals obtained by resampling the 30 pipelines. The closed-form comparison and
the fusion replay were added after the run and are reported as post-hoc analyses.

\textbf{Attack capability.} With a public, fixed schedule, a precomputed phase attack reaches its
target undetected on \FixedU{} of \TotalN{} attempts; with secret interrogation times it succeeds
on \HiddenU{}. A phase table optimized against the secret schedule shifts the estimate in
\OptimizedT{} attempts but passes the verifier in only \OptimizedU{}; this is a lower bound on
attacker capability, since the search is finite. A drive-frequency offset succeeds undetected on
\FrequencyU{} of \TotalN{} attempts even with secret timing, and simulated count distributions
match the field-shifted ones to within $\EquivalenceError$.

\textbf{Fallback reaches the reference floor.} At the nominal operating point, combined fallback
releases a harmful estimate on \NominalHarmRate\% [\NominalHarmCI] of attacks, identical to
always-reference; this rate is the reference's own error at $\sigma_R=20$\,nT, not an effect the
alarm can remove. A 50\,nT spoof lies in the transition region and raises harm to
\SmallHarmRate\%. A noisier reference with $\sigma_R=50$\,nT widens $\kappa$ until the 100\,nT spoof
falls inside the blind window: fallback harm rises to \WeakHarmRate\%, worse than the reference
alone (\WeakReferenceHarmRate\%). A precise reference with $\sigma_R=5$\,nT removes harm entirely.
Fig.~\ref{fig:theory} shows that Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed}, evaluated with the calibrated thresholds
and no fitting to attack data, matches all \TheoryConditions{} tested conditions ($\sigma_R\in\{5,15,20,50\}$\,nT, a 50\,nT spoof,
a 10\,nT reference bias, and $\lambda\in\{0.5,1\}$) within \TheoryMaxGap{} percentage points.

\section{Design Rules and Limitations}
\label{sec:rules}

Table~\ref{tab:hw} maps each countermeasure to what it stops and the hardware it needs; the results
reduce to four rules for sensor designers. First, match the defense to the actuator: secret timing
costs only scheduler logic and stops precomputed phase attacks, but not frequency offsets. Second,
size the reference to the application's tolerance, not to the detector's alarm rate: for a 25\,nT
tolerance the reference noise must stay below \TheorySigmaCritical\,nT, or small spoofs pass through
the blind window. Third, audit the reference for shared clocks, synthesizers and data paths, since
a shared clock turns the reference into a second victim. Fourth, prefer drive schemes that address
both spin transitions, which cancel common clock errors at the cost of a second tone. Integrity
monitoring in satellite navigation follows the same logic of separating fault detection from fault
exclusion~\cite{brown1992raim,psiaki2016gnss}.

All results are from simulation. The reference is an abstract Gaussian channel without drift,
bandwidth or alignment effects; the attacker is modeled at the level of frequency and phase
offsets rather than as a circuit-level synthesizer or clock model; the attacker's phase-table
search is finite; and the 30 pipelines replicate sampling and calibration variance within one
code base.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A single-transition NV Ramsey magnetometer cannot tell a sub-ppm microwave drive offset from a
real field change, so its integrity depends on hardware outside the spin. An independent reference
can detect the attack, but under attack its own error rate is the best that fallback can achieve,
and a noisy
reference opens a blind window in which small spoofs pass unnoticed. The closed-form design
equation, which matches the simulation, lets designers size the reference to the
application's tolerance, and a dependency audit shows that sharing a clock with the primary
sensor removes the protection entirely. Future work will simulate dual-transition sensors under
two-tone attacks, model the clock and synthesizer at circuit level, and demonstrate drive spoofing
and reference-assisted verification on NV hardware.
